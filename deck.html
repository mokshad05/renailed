Problem · Model · Money · Pilot · Impact

One house, then the region, kept as separate datasets. Grandview-Woodland is a City of Vancouver deconstruction case study: 13,005 kg of lumber and 160 hours of de-nailing on that job; the case sheet does not say how much of the lumber those hours covered. Later slides divide 160 hours by the 7.15 tonnes reused to get 22.4 hours per reused tonne; that is our assumption. The $15,310 is the City’s deposit on demolition permits for pre-1950 houses. It belongs to the contractor, and the City refunds it on a sliding scale when the contractor proves reuse and recycling. It is not ReNailed revenue. Regionally, Metro Vancouver’s 2022 study found 177,011 tonnes of wood in disposed construction and demolition waste, 48 percent by weight. Separately, Riley Park was taken apart by hand: of its 24,120 kilograms of lumber, 22 percent was reused, 74 percent went to waste-to-energy and 4 percent to landfill. So 96 percent avoided landfill, but only 22 percent kept its value as wood. Some of that reused lumber went into the Welcome Parlour in North Vancouver.

Problem · Model · Money · Pilot · Impact

This is the core of the business. Sort and Supply is a paid recovery service for demolition contractors, with furniture as the premium outlet for the best short pieces. The contractor pays a service fee, which is untested. We run a proposed screen on every lot: the City’s hazardous-materials report, then copper and lead screens. We sort into our own categories, not certified lumber grades: A is clean lumber six feet and longer, for reclaimed yards such as Western Reclaimed and Heritage Lumber, which bought Dunbar’s lumber at about $1,132 a tonne, or donation to Habitat; B is clean wood from two feet to just under six, for benches and tables; P is sixteen inches to just under two feet, for planters; C is shorter clean scraps for Smallwood, where mulch counts as recycling but hog fuel is energy recovery, so we will confirm the City’s credit for each route. Metal goes to a scrap yard such as ABC Recycling. Painted and treated wood goes to the Vancouver Landfill at $124 a tonne, creosote to Summit Earthworks. Every destination is potential until it accepts a load. Receipts support the contractor’s deposit claim, but the City decides the refund. Reused wood counts five times its weight toward the 75 percent target; recycling counts once; landfill counts nothing. Our advantage: yards buy long wood and recyclers take scraps, but no one sorts every load, including small lots, and documents every destination.

Appendix D · Judge Q&A

The eight hardest questions, answered

1 · Who has agreed to pay $3,500?

No one yet. $3,500 [T] matches Richmond’s dedication fee; our first milestone is one letter of intent.

2 · What stops a copycat?

The design is copyable. Contractor access, City-ready receipts and grade data take time. Best case: they buy our slats.

3 · Do you make money?

Sorting: ≈ $62/t known so far at 22% reuse [C], before fees and quotes. Benches: no margin claimed until frame and install are quoted.

4 · How strong is salvaged wood?

One US test of 1940s Douglas-fir found ≈ 25% lower bending strength (Teshnizi). Our stress estimate needs an engineer and a load test.

5 · How do you keep out toxic wood?

City hazardous-materials report, then proposed copper and lead screens. Positive or painted wood is rejected.

6 · Most wood is painted or treated. Then what?

≈ 74% of disposed dimensional lumber [C] (MV 2022 study). Never reused: landfill at $124/t; creosote to Summit Earthworks.

7 · Does a new steel frame cancel the benefit?

Possibly: steel averages 1.92 t CO₂ per tonne (worldsteel, 2023). No carbon claim until we weigh the frame and run a life-cycle study.

8 · What result makes you stop?

Stop or revise sorting if confirmed income can’t cover all measured costs. A product design that fails its load test is withdrawn.

Sources: Richmond; Burnaby Now; Teshnizi (2015); USDA Wood Handbook; Health Canada; MV C&D study 2022; worldsteel. [T] assumption · [C] our calculation.

Long, sound pieces can go to benches or, only after a CLSAB-accredited agency re-grades and stamps them, to scaffolds, because WorkSafeBC section 13.16 requires all scaffold lumber to be graded and marked to NLGA rules. We found no source that allows painted or treated reclaimed wood in scaffolds, and Health Canada warns that homes built before 1960 probably have lead paint and that decks and fences built before 2004 are likely CCA-treated, so neither is reused for anything. To spot treated wood, the desk uses a PAN indicator stain: Florida's environment department says it turns magenta to red on copper-treated wood, including CCA,. Paint and nails can give a false positive, so a positive board is always treated as contaminated. Painted and treated wood goes to the Vancouver Landfill at $124 a tonne; the landfill does not accept creosote-treated wood. Creosote goes to Summit Earthworks' Mission Riverside facility. We no longer list Lafarge: Metro Vancouver's recycling directory lists the Lafarge and Geocycle Richmond site as clean only. Clean plywood and OSB go to Habitat for Humanity Greater Vancouver's ReStore, which accepts sheet goods. Our frames use new steel. Salvaged steel has no mill certificate, which Densley Tingley and colleagues describe as a key barrier to reusing structural steel, so we send it to licensed metal recyclers rather than assume its strength. Steel cannot become scaffold parts, because WorkSafeBC sections 13.2 and 13.15 require components that meet a CSA or ANSI standard and follow manufacturer or engineer data. All salvaged metal, including the nails we pull, goes to licensed metal recyclers.

Appendix H · Operations: access, process, build, maintain

Access, process, build, maintain

1 · How will we access the wood?

Pre-demolition survey with the contractor’s consent. Pre-1950 houses need 75–90% reuse or recycling and a $15,310 deposit, refunded on a sliding scale; reused wood counts 5× its weight (By-law 11023). The wood becomes ours at pickup at no charge; the contractor separately pays a service fee [T] and gets receipts.

2 · How will we process it?

Proposed process: copper and lead screens, metal-scan, pull nails, grade nail holes as knots (Falk & Green), accept ≤ 19% moisture (CWC), mill. Benchmark: 160 h for 7.15 t reused (CoV), ≈ 35 min per bench [T]. Kit cost: quote needed.

3 · Who will build and install it?

We plan to assemble slats on a local fabricator’s steel frame [T]; an engineer sets the load test; an installer anchors it. No fabricator or installer is confirmed yet.

4 · How will we maintain it?

Bolted slats swap out. Burnaby budgets $200 per bench per year for upkeep (Burnaby Now); our plan price is not set [T]. Fir is “moderately resistant to decay” (Wood Handbook), so slats drain and worn ones are recycled (MV).

These are the four operating questions our mentor asked of every stakeholder map, applied to ReNailed. Access works because the City’s deposit and recycling rules give contractors a reason to say yes, and disposal costs them money; reused wood counts five times its weight toward their target under section 4.1 of the Green Demolition By-law. Processing is benchmarked against City case-study data, with our assumption that the 160 hours covered the 7.15 tonnes reused. Building would use paid suppliers, none confirmed, and an engineer sets the tests. The wood itself becomes ours at no charge at pickup, separate from the contractor’s service fee, which is untested.
