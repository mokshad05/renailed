Problem · Model · Money · Pilot · Impact

This is the core of the business. Sort and Supply is a paid recovery service for demolition contractors. The Material ID Desk screens each lot after the City’s hazardous-materials report, with a copper spot test and a lead check. We sort into grades: A is clean lumber six feet and longer, sold to reclaimed yards or donated to the ReStore; B is clean two-to-six-foot wood, our furniture supply; C is clean scraps for recycling; metal goes to scrap yards; painted and treated wood is rejected and disposed of. The De-Nail Lab logs every minute and kilo, because labour is our biggest cost. Every load ends in a receipt. Furniture is where select pieces earn the most, not the whole company. Our advantage over existing players: reclaimed yards buy long wood, recyclers take scraps and Urbanjacks finger-joins short pieces, but we sort and grade every load, including small lots, and give the contractor a receipt. We would rather supply those players than compete with them.

This is the material flow and who pays at each step. The contractor pays us a service fee, which is untested, and gets a receipt for every load toward the City's 75 percent reuse-and-recycling target and their deposit of up to $15,310, refunded on a sliding scale. The desk screens and grades. Grade A, clean lumber six feet and longer, goes to reclaimed yards, which paid about $1,132 a tonne in the one Dunbar sale we have, or is donated to Habitat's ReStore. Grade B, clean two-to-six-foot pieces, becomes our furniture, paid per product by campuses, cafés and gardens; that price is not yet tested. Grade C, clean scraps, goes to a clean-wood recycler such as Smallwood; we need its price. Metal goes to licensed scrap yards such as ABC Recycling, which takes construction and demolition scrap; we need a quote. The contractor owns the material until pickup; then it is ours to sell. We sort on site where there is space, otherwise at a partner yard, which is still an assumption. We pay transport, storage and screening, and the cost of disposing of rejects is built into the service fee. Rejects cost money: painted or treated wood goes to the Vancouver Landfill at $124 a tonne, and creosote to Summit Earthworks. Reused wood counts five times its weight under section 4.1 of the by-law; recycling counts once; landfill counts nothing.

Appendix E · Judge Q&A

The eight hardest questions, answered

1 · Who has agreed to pay $3,500?

No one yet. $3,500 [T] matches Richmond’s dedication fee; our first milestone is one letter of intent.

2 · What stops a copycat?

The design is copyable. Contractor access, City-ready receipts and grade data take time. Best case: they buy our slats.

3 · Do you make money?

Sorting: ≈ $62/t known so far at 22% reuse [C], before fees and quotes. Benches: no margin claimed until frame and install are quoted.

4 · How strong is salvaged wood?

About 25% weaker than new (Teshnizi). Supports every 2 ft keep stress ≈ 5 MPa vs ≈ 85 MPa for clear fir [C]. Seating only.

5 · How do you keep out toxic wood?

City hazardous-materials report, then a copper spot test. Painted and pre-2004 deck or fence wood (CCA) is rejected.

6 · Most wood is painted or treated. Then what?

≈ 88% of disposed lumber [C] (Tetra Tech). Never reused: landfill at $124/t; creosote to Summit Earthworks.

7 · Does a new steel frame cancel the benefit?

Possibly: a 25 kg frame [T] ≈ 48 kg CO₂ [C] (worldsteel). No carbon claim until a life-cycle study.

8 · What result makes you stop?

No buyer above our quoted cost in five talks, reused lumber under ≈ $848/t [C], or a failed load test. Pivot: sell graded slats.

Sources: Richmond; Burnaby Now; Teshnizi (2015); USDA Wood Handbook; Health Canada; Tetra Tech (2026); worldsteel. [T] assumption · [C] our calculation.

The eight questions judges are most likely to ask. 1: Richmond charges $3,500 for a 10-year dedication bench; Surrey $2,800 to $3,800; no buyer has agreed yet. 2: our moat is access, receipts and data, not the design. 3: 2.2 tonnes reused per 10-tonne lot at $1,132 a tonne, minus de-nailing at minimum wage and worst-case landfill, leaves about $624; product costs need quotes. 4: salvaged wood is about 25 percent weaker; recycled 2x4s average one grade lower (Falk and Green). 5: painted wood before 1960 may contain lead; deck and fence wood before 2004 may be CCA-treated; the copper spot test follows Florida DEP. 6: Tetra Tech Table D-1, 55,695 tonnes stained or treated, 9,271 painted, 9,074 clean. 7: worldsteel's 1.92 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of steel. 8: any one of these stops us; $848 a tonne is the break-even price for reused lumber before quotes. This model uses only inputs we can source, and one reuse rate everywhere: Riley Park’s 22 percent, the lowest of the City’s case studies; Grandview-Woodland reused 55 percent, so this is the conservative case. Reused lumber is priced at the one Dunbar sale, $6,000 for 5.3 tonnes, about $1,132 a tonne, assuming all of it was sold. De-nailing takes about 22.4 hours a tonne, from Grandview-Woodland’s 160 hours for 7.15 tonnes reused. Labour is costed at BC’s minimum wage of $18.25, the legal floor, so real labour will cost more. Disposal is the Vancouver Landfill’s 2026 wood rate of $124 a tonne. On a 10-tonne lot that leaves about $624 before the contractor fee, metal and scrap income, transport and storage, which all need quotes. Break-even for reused lumber is about $848 a tonne. For a bench, only the wood and de-nailing, about $40, are sourced; the frame, installation and engineering need quotes, so we show no product margin and no annual profit until we have them. Judges can scan the code and add their own numbers.

The Material ID Desk now sorts metal as well as wood. Clean wood means unpainted, unstained, untreated and free of glue, per Metro Vancouver's clean-wood card, and dry lumber is 19 percent moisture or less, per the Canadian Wood Council. Short clean pieces become slats and planter boards. Long, sound pieces can go to benches or, only after a CLSAB-accredited agency re-grades and stamps them, to scaffolds, because WorkSafeBC section 13.16 requires all scaffold lumber to be graded and marked to NLGA rules. We found no source that allows painted or treated reclaimed wood in scaffolds, and Health Canada warns that homes built before 1960 probably have lead paint and that decks and fences built before 2004 are likely CCA-treated, so neither is reused for anything. To spot treated wood, the desk uses a PAN indicator stain: Florida's environment department says it turns magenta to red on copper-treated wood, including CCA, in about 15 seconds, for a few cents a test. Paint and nails can give a false positive, so a positive board is always treated as contaminated. Painted and treated wood goes to the Vancouver Landfill at $124 a tonne; the City accepts painted, stained or treated wood in loads up to 3 tonnes, free of metal hardware, but not creosote. Creosote goes to Summit Earthworks' Mission Riverside facility. We no longer list Lafarge: Metro Vancouver's licence B034 for Lafarge's Richmond recovery site accepts only clean wood not treated with preservatives. Clean plywood and OSB go to Habitat for Humanity Greater Vancouver's ReStore, which accepts sheet goods. Our frames use new steel. Salvaged steel has no mill certificate, which Densley Tingley and colleagues describe as a key barrier to reusing structural steel, so we send it to licensed metal recyclers rather than assume its strength. Steel cannot become scaffold parts, because WorkSafeBC sections 13.2 and 13.15 require components that meet a CSA or ANSI standard and follow manufacturer or engineer data. All salvaged metal, including the nails we pull, goes to licensed metal recyclers. Metro Vancouver's 2025 study found 14,072 tonnes of ferrous metal in disposed construction and demolition waste.

A judge may ask: most of the wood is painted or treated, so why not build a big plant for it? Demolite in Finland shows the model: it receives treated wood, chips it, pulls out the nails and screws for recycling, and sells uniform chips to plants that hold a permit to burn treated wood for electricity, heat and steam. Enva in Manchester handles up to 500 tonnes of wood a day, but only non-hazardous grades. The lesson is that the yard prepares the wood and a permitted plant burns it. In BC we have not found that plant yet. Lafarge Richmond's licence takes clean wood only. Lafarge's Exshaw plant in Alberta takes up to 120,000 tonnes a year of construction and demolition wood through aggregators like ECCO Recycling, but its page does not say treated wood, and shipping from Vancouver needs a freight quote. Kruger's Kamloops mill burns hog fuel and is short of fibre, but nothing says it takes demolition wood. Smallwood in Surrey takes untreated wood only. Metro Vancouver's waste-to-energy plant burns about 250,000 tonnes of garbage a year and publishes no wood rules, so we would ask. Summit Earthworks in Mission takes creosote. Brooks, Alberta proposed gasifying CP rail ties, but it is not built. So our rule is: get written acceptance from a permitted plant before buying any shredding equipment, and site the yard near that plant.
