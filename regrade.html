(35 s) The City of Vancouver says most pre-1950 homes are built of old-growth lumber. In the City’s Riley Park case study, one was taken apart by hand. Only 22 percent of its lumber was reused as wood; 74 percent was burned for energy. Across the region, wood is nearly half of the construction and demolition waste that gets disposed: 48 percent in Metro Vancouver’s 2022 study. Good wood is leaving the system without keeping its value.

(45 s) ReGrade is a paid sorting service for demolition contractors; furniture is the premium outlet. Every lot is screened first: the City’s hazardous-materials report, a copper test and a lead check. We de-nail and sell the nails and metal to a scrap yard, grade the wood by length, send every grade to a buyer, and give the contractor a receipt for every load, which supports their deposit claim. Three payers: contractors pay a service fee, still untested; yards and recyclers buy wood and metal; product buyers pay per item.

(40 s) Every piece of a teardown has a place to go. Long boards, 6 feet and up, are Grade A: reclaimed yards such as Western Reclaimed and Heritage Lumber, or Habitat for Humanity. Pieces from 2 to 6 feet are Grade B and become our benches and tables; 16-inch offcuts become planters. Clean scraps go to a wood recycler such as Smallwood, nails and metal to a scrap yard such as ABC Recycling, and painted or treated wood to approved disposal, never reuse. Every load gets a receipt the contractor can use to prove compliance.

(35 s) Impact is why ReGrade exists. Every lot we grade works toward two UN Sustainable Development Goals. Goal 12, responsible consumption and production: target 12.5 asks for less waste through reuse, and we keep good wood in use as wood; target 12.4 asks for safe handling of waste, and we screen out treated wood on every lot. Goal 11, sustainable cities: target 11.6 asks cities to cut their waste impact, and we sort and receipt every teardown. We stay honest about it: we count only reuse, and we track recycling, energy recovery and landfill separately. Riley Park’s waste-to-energy share is energy recovery, not diversion we created, and we make no carbon claim until we run a life-cycle method.

Appendix · Q&A

Appendix A · Judge questions 1–4

Customers, safety, buyers and money

1 · Why would a contractor pay you?

Their $15,310 deposit is refunded on proven reuse and recycling. Our receipts document every load, and reused wood counts 5× toward the 75% target. The fee is untested [T]; the pilot prices it.

2 · Is the wood safe?

Every lot gets the City’s hazmat report, a copper test and a lead check. Painted and treated wood is never reused. No product is sold before a load test.

3 · Who buys the wood?

Grade A goes to reclaimed yards (Heritage Lumber paid ≈ $1,132/t for Dunbar’s lumber) or Habitat. Grade B becomes our products. Scraps and metal: Smallwood and ABC Recycling, quotes pending.

4 · Do you make money?

About +$624 is known per 10-tonne lot [C], before the fee, metal income and collection. Break-even is ≈ $848/t. Four written quotes will settle it.

[T] untested assumption · [C] our calculation. Sources as on the main slides.

Short answers to the four questions judges are most likely to ask first: why contractors pay, safety, who buys the wood, and whether the numbers work.

Appendix · Q&A

Appendix A · Judge questions 5–8

Labour, products, impact and the stop rule

5 · How long does de-nailing take?

22.4 h per reused tonne in the City’s Grandview-Woodland case, costed at BC’s $18.25/h minimum wage as a floor.

6 · Why make furniture at all?

It gives Grade B pieces a higher-value outlet. City dedication fees ($2,800–$9,500) are anchors, not prices. No margin claimed until a fabricator quotes the frame.

7 · What impact do you claim?

Only reuse. Recycling, energy recovery and landfill are reported separately. No carbon claim until a life-cycle method is run.

8 · What if the pilot fails?

We stop. We go only if the measured lot is positive at quoted prices; otherwise we revise the grades, fee or routes before trying again.

Sources: CoV Grandview-Woodland case study; BC Employment Standards; Surrey, Richmond and Vancouver dedication programs.

Four more likely questions: labour time, why furniture, what impact we claim, and what happens if the pilot does not work.

Zoom out. Metro Vancouver’s 2022 study found 177,011 tonnes of wood in disposed construction and demolition waste, 48 percent of it. In Vancouver, pre-1950 houses must reach 75 percent reuse or recycling, and reused wood counts five times its weight toward that target under By-law 11023. The contractor pays a $15,310 deposit and gets it back on a sliding scale when they prove compliance with receipts. That refund is the contractor’s money, not ours. A May 2025 Council motion noted there is no similar reuse rule yet for post-1950 homes.

Safety is a mandatory constraint, not a pilot step we can trade off. Every lot gets the City’s hazardous-materials report, a copper test and a lead check before we touch the wood. Painted and treated wood is never reused; it goes to approved disposal. And no product is sold before a load test. Material safety, what we accept, and product safety, what we sell, are two separate checks.
